Museum Audio Guide Narration Exercises

Guide a visitor from an object they can find to a detail they can observe and a fact the exhibit supports. The routine tests looking time, directional clarity, and separation of verified interpretation from speculation. It is object-led narration rather than a general documentary read.

Start the six-drill routineRead the original example

Controlled practice

Work through the routine one drill at a time.

Change one variable, listen back, then mark the drill. Progress stays on this device and carries across the language switch. The checklist stores completion flags, never recordings.

1

Locate the object

Describe an unambiguous exhibit label or visible landmark. Check the description against the visitor’s position rather than assuming they face the same direction as you.

2

Observe before explaining

Invite the visitor to notice one visible detail before describing its meaning. Keep the observation and the interpretation in separate sentences.

3

Fact-source marking

Mark every date, attribution, material, and historical claim with its approved source. Read uncertainty explicitly when the exhibit does not establish a single answer.

4

Looking-time pass

Leave time for a visitor to find the detail. Record a version that rushes and another that allows looking, then test them beside the object.

5

Pronunciation approval

Make a short list of names and specialist terms. Resolve pronunciations with the exhibit owner instead of guessing a confident version during recording.

6

Next-stop handoff

End with the next action or stop identifier. Test whether someone can follow it without walking back or losing their place in the guide.

0 of 6 drills completed. Self-review, not an audio score.

Original practice card

Use one fixed example for a fair comparison.

Use a fictional exhibit card: “Study bowl, maker unknown, practice object.” Read: “Find the shallow bowl marked A. Notice the three grooves near the rim. The maker is unknown. When you are ready, continue to practice stop B.” No historical date or origin is supplied, so the narrator must not invent one.

How should the first pass work?

Read the example and limits before starting, keep a baseline, and work through the six drills above. Make two takes with a recording device you control and note the specific change privately. On playback, check that information remains intact before judging expressive choices. Stop if uncomfortable; no audio upload is needed.

Observable result

Judge the result on playback.

Pass when a visitor locates the correct object, finds the stated detail, distinguishes facts from unknowns, hears approved names clearly, and knows the next stop. Reject a fluent take if it adds provenance absent from the card. Note the moment a listener needed more viewing time and adjust that pause only.

A checked box records that you completed a step. It does not certify voice quality, skill, or professional readiness.

Limits and failure cases

Repair a failed comparison before continuing.

Production copy requires curatorial approval and feedback from intended visitors. This practice card is fictional and carries no museum attribution. Directional instructions must be checked on site; they cannot establish that a route is accessible or safe.

Too many simultaneous changes

Changing words, level, position, and pace together makes the comparison inconclusive. Return to the example baseline and retain only the variable specified by the current drill.

Impressions instead of evidence

A more attractive sound does not establish that the task worked. Apply the page-specific playback checks, and ask a listener unfamiliar with the example to recount the information before keeping a take.

Effort used to force completion

The completion record is not an endurance contest. Stop at discomfort, shorten the session, and return to comfortable speech; pain or excessive vocal effort is never a condition for checking a box.
