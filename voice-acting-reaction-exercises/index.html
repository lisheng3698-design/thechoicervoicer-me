The Choicer Voicer/Voice acting reaction exercises

6 drills · 16 minutes · Event before answer

Voice acting reaction exercises that begin with the cue.

A convincing reaction is evidence that something just happened to the character. Instead of decorating the reply, receive one exact cue, name what changed, allow the smallest necessary processing beat, and carry the consequence through the next words.

Start the six-drill routineStrengthen cue listening

Receive, change, answer

Make the event audible without indicating it.

Record this cue first: “The train left ten minutes ago.” Your response is: “Then we use the service road.”

1

Fact baseline

Play the cue, name the literal new fact, and deliver the reply without adding an emotion or reaction sound.

2

Expectation break

Before playback, expect the train to wait. Let the word “left” replace that expectation before your response begins.

3

Latency ladder

Record an immediate reply, a one-beat reply, and a two-beat reply. Keep the event specific and compare which pause is earned.

4

Silent first response

Ban sighs, gasps, and fillers. Show the change only through breath timing, entry, operative word, and sentence ending.

5

Consequence carry

Add: “Call Mara and move the cases.” Keep the original event active through both instructions rather than resetting after line one.

6

Cue variation test

Replace “ten minutes” with “ten seconds” and then “two hours.” Let urgency come from the changed fact, not a louder preset.

Reaction equation

Track expectation, event, and consequence

Write three short clauses: “I expected…,” “I learned…,” and “Now I must….” This prevents a vague reaction such as “more shocked.” The same cue can produce relief, calculation, denial, or urgency because different characters expected different outcomes and need different next actions.

What should change on playback?

Listen to the first breath or consonant after the cue, the word that carries the new plan, and whether the consequence survives into the following sentence. A large gasp followed by an unchanged read is decoration, not a completed reaction.

Pass the drill

Five response checks

  • The exact cue fact is received.
  • A previous expectation is named.
  • Pause length matches processing.
  • The next action follows the event.
  • No automatic gasp is required.

Pass with four of five, including cue and consequence.

Avoid indicating

Three reactions that lose the event

The most useful response is caused, not displayed.

Preloading emotion

If the actor decides to be shocked before the cue, the incoming words cannot create a new thought.

Automatic vocal noise

A sigh or gasp on every cue becomes a habit. Earn nonverbal sound from the event and keep it comfortable.

Resetting after one word

The consequence should shape the whole answer, not disappear after an expressive first syllable.

Transfer

Receive the reference before recording

In The Choicer Voicer game, play the reference and identify one piece of information that changes the character. Use relationship exercises to specify who delivered it, then review the first second of your response with self critique.

Is a voice acting reaction always a sound?

No. A reaction can be a changed breath, delayed start, redirected word, unfinished phrase, or new sentence shape. Add a vocal sound only when the event calls for one.

How long should I pause before replying?

Long enough for the specific thought change and no longer. Compare immediate, one-beat, and two-beat versions, then choose the shortest pause that preserves the event.

Quick start

Expectation, event, action

Write the three clauses, play the cue once, and record the shortest response that makes the change clear.
