The Choicer Voicer/Voice acting status exercises

6 drills · 18 minutes · Relationship power

Voice acting status exercises for audible power shifts.

Status is the moment-to-moment permission a character believes they have—not a fixed label for wealth, job, or worth. These drills keep the words constant while you change certainty, space, interruption, deference, and the exact beat where power moves.

Start the six-drill routinePrepare the private thought

Permission, not pitch

Change one status behavior at a time.

Use the original line: “I thought you might want to see this before the meeting.” Keep your comfortable voice and recording level.

1

Neutral transaction

Name the listener and record a factual baseline. Neither character needs approval; the aim is a stable comparison take.

2

Permission contrast

Deliver the line once as if you may place the object down, then as if you must wait for permission. Change starts and endings, not loudness.

3

Time ownership

Give the listener unlimited time, then only three seconds. Hear whether pauses feel granted, borrowed, or deliberately withheld.

4

Interruption recovery

Imagine the listener cuts in after “want.” Resume as high status without rushing, then as low status by repairing and checking permission.

5

Status seesaw

Begin certain, discover on “this” that the evidence is weak, and finish exposed. Reverse the shift on a second take.

6

Hidden-number test

Record status levels two, five, and eight without naming them. Shuffle playback and ask whether a listener can order the three takes.

Behavior map

Use evidence instead of a generic “powerful voice”

Before each take, write one permission statement: “I can make them wait,” “I need their approval,” or “I just gained leverage.” Then choose one audible behavior—entry timing, pause ownership, directness, repair, sentence ending, or amount of explanation. A high-status character may be quiet because they expect attention; a low-status character may be loud because they fear losing it.

What should change on playback?

Check whether the line begins by claiming or requesting space, whether pauses feel chosen or accidental, and whether the final word closes the exchange or invites approval. If only pitch or volume changes, repeat with a clearer relationship rule.

Pass the drill

Five audible checks

  • The listener and permission rule are specific.
  • One behavior changes between takes.
  • The words and mic distance stay constant.
  • The status turn lands on a marked word.
  • No identity stereotype defines rank.

Pass with four of five, including the stereotype check.

Avoid shortcuts

Three choices that flatten status

Status lives in a relationship and can change faster than a character voice.

High equals deep

A lower pitch can become a costume. Test patience, brevity, and permission before changing vocal placement.

Low equals weak

Deference is an action, not a judgment. A character can yield social space while remaining precise and determined.

One level all scene

New evidence, an interruption, or a changed goal can move status inside a single sentence.

Transfer

Put the status turn into a playable scene

Choose a cue in The Choicer Voicer game, name who controls time at the start, and mark the word that could change that control. Use character relationship exercises to define history, then use self critique to test whether the turn is audible without an explanation.

Does high status mean speaking loudly?

No. High status may sound still, economical, patient, or certain, while low status may become fast, careful, permission-seeking, or over-explanatory.

Can status change during one voice acting line?

Yes. Mark the word where new information changes who has permission, certainty, or control, then let timing and sentence shape reveal the turn.

Quick start

One line, three permissions

Record as if you own the time, share the time, then must borrow the time. Keep volume and words stable.
