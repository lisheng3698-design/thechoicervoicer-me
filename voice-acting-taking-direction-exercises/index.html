The Choicer Voicer/Voice acting taking direction exercises

6 drills · 20 minutes · Directed retakes

Voice acting taking direction exercises for cleaner retakes.

A useful retake is not just “different.” It preserves the script and session facts while changing the behavior named by the note. This routine converts broad phrases such as “more energy” or “less announcer” into listener, action, timing, and intensity choices another person can compare.

Start the direction routineSet a playback baseline

Preserve, then change

Turn one note into one audible variable.

Use the original line: “The package arrived earlier than we expected.” Label every take before recording.

1

Baseline contract

Record a clear first take. Write what must remain: words, character facts, mic distance, and the listener.

2

Translate “more energy”

Choose one meaning: decide sooner, shorten recovery pauses, or pursue the listener more actively. Record only that change.

3

Translate “less announcer”

Move from presenting to one person toward helping that person notice the early arrival. Keep clarity without smoothing every phrase.

4

Opposite-note pair

Record “protect the listener” and “challenge the listener.” Compare the behavioral contrast without changing the character voice.

5

Resolve a conflict

Combine “urgent” with “do not rush” by deciding earlier while preserving space between ideas. State the solution in one sentence.

6

Cold retake

Wait one minute, draw one note at random, and deliver within ten seconds. Check whether the requested variable changed and the rest survived.

Direction translator

Convert adjectives into production choices

For each note, choose one category: listener, action, information, timing, intensity, or physical circumstance. “Warmer” might mean protect the listener from bad news; “bigger” might mean the listener is farther away; “faster” might mean the thought arrives sooner rather than every syllable shrinking.

When should you ask a question?

Ask when two plausible choices would lead to opposite takes. Use a compact either/or: “Should the urgency come from the deadline or from hiding the news?” Then record the answer instead of debating terminology.

Pass the retake

Five direction checks

  • The baseline is labeled and saved.
  • One variable answers the note.
  • Words and session facts survive.
  • The contrast is audible on playback.
  • Clarification is concise and playable.

Pass with four of five, including preservation.

Stay directable

Three habits that waste a retake

Direction is shared problem-solving, not a test of mind reading.

Changing everything

A new pace, pitch, accent, listener, and objective make it impossible to tell which choice answered the note.

Copying the note's sound

Do not imitate the director's delivery. Translate the idea into behavior that belongs to the character and scene.

Explaining before trying

When the note is playable, acknowledge it and record. Save questions for ambiguity that would materially split the take.

Transfer

Practice a session without pretending to know the answer

Draw a prompt from The Choicer Voicer game, make a stable first read with line delivery exercises, and use audition exercises for a time-limited alternate. Afterward, use self critique to identify whether the note—not general polish—became clearer.

Should a voice actor ask questions about direction?

Yes, when one concise question resolves a real ambiguity. Offer two playable interpretations rather than asking for a complete explanation of the scene.

Does taking direction mean imitating the director's voice?

No. Translate the note into listener, action, timing, intensity, or physical circumstance while keeping the performance truthful in your own instrument.

Quick start

One note, one variable

Record the package line, then make “more urgent” change only the speed of decisions—not the word rate.
