The Choicer Voicer/Voice acting warm-up exercises

8 minutes · Gentle preparation · No equipment

Voice acting warm-up exercises before a scene.

Prepare posture, easy breath, lips, tongue, resonance, articulation, and comfortable pitch movement in eight short stages. The goal is readiness and clarity—not maximum range or volume.

Warm up, then playStart minute one

Minutes 1–4

Release effort before making bigger choices.

Stand or sit with balanced support. Keep every sound at an easy conversational level.

1

Align and release

Let shoulders settle, soften the jaw, and turn the head gently within a comfortable range. Avoid large neck circles.

2

Easy breath

Inhale without lifting the shoulders and exhale on a quiet “sss.” Repeat three times without chasing a longer breath.

3

Lips and tongue

Alternate relaxed lip trills, “brr,” and slow tongue movement behind the teeth. Skip any motion that feels awkward.

4

Gentle hum

Hum “mm” on one easy pitch, then open to “mee” without adding pressure. Notice vibration rather than forcing it.

Minutes 5–8

5. Comfortable glides

Use a lip trill or light “ng” to glide a small distance upward and back down. Keep the movement well inside your easy range. Two or three smooth glides are enough; the warm-up is not a test of extreme notes.

6. Articulation ladder

Speak “mee–may–mah–moh–moo” slowly, then “bee–dee–gee.” Aim for clear beginnings and relaxed transitions. Add a simple phrase such as “bright blue balloons” first slowly and then at normal speech pace.

7. Energy scale

Say “I found the answer” at three safe levels: private thought, conversation, and calling across a small room. Keep pitch and pace stable so energy is the only variable. Do not shout.

8. Scene check

Choose one short line. Speak it neutrally, then with one playable action such as warning, welcoming, or persuading. If both takes feel free and repeatable, begin the session. If the second adds tension, reduce the choice rather than pushing through.

The eight-minute timer

  1. One minute: posture and jaw release.
  2. One minute: easy breath on “sss.”
  3. One minute: lips and tongue.
  4. One minute: gentle humming.
  5. One minute: small pitch glides.
  6. One minute: articulation ladder.
  7. One minute: three energy levels.
  8. One minute: neutral and acted scene check.

Adjust the routine to the task

For a quiet conversational scene, spend more time on clear consonants and less on room-calling energy. For an animated character, keep the same gentle foundation and explore a slightly wider—but still comfortable—pitch and energy pattern. For a voice imitation game, add one rhythm skeleton and one phrase-contour pass.

When to stop

A warm-up should not cause pain, dizziness, persistent coughing, increasing hoarseness, or lasting tightness. Stop when a drill feels uncomfortable. If symptoms persist or you have an existing voice concern, seek guidance from an appropriate qualified professional rather than relying on this general practice page.

Move from warm-up to feedback

After warming up, record a single short phrase and listen at normal volume. Check whether words start cleanly, pauses remain intentional, and pitch movement feels easy. The progressive imitation routine shows how to add one layer at a time.

Warm-up mistakes to avoid

  • Trying to expand range quickly.
  • Using loudness as proof that the voice is ready.
  • Repeating tongue twisters faster than clarity allows.
  • Continuing after pain or increasing strain.
  • Changing every vocal variable in the first scene.

Safety boundary

Easy and repeatable

  • No forced breath holds
  • No maximum-volume calls
  • No extreme pitch targets
  • No movement through pain
  • Water as needed
