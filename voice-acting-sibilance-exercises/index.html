Voice Acting Sibilance Exercises

Compare sharp sibilant sounds with matched recordings before applying processing. Keep words intelligible while testing microphone angle, distance, and playback conditions. The goal is an evidence-based recording adjustment, with a clear distinction between sound texture and speech correction.

Start the six-drill routineRead the original example

Controlled practice

Work through the routine one drill at a time.

Change one variable, listen back, then mark the drill. Progress stays on this device and carries across the language switch. The checklist stores completion flags, never recordings.

1

Unprocessed baseline

Record the same short line without a de-esser or noise effect. Mark which words sound unusually sharp and the listening device used for that judgment.

2

Angle-only pass

Change microphone angle slightly while keeping other variables stable. Compare the sharp words and check whether ordinary vowels have become duller.

3

Distance-only pass

Return to a known angle and test a small distance change. Match playback loudness before deciding that a softer take has less sibilance.

4

Device comparison

Listen on headphones and a second ordinary speaker. Note whether the problem appears on both instead of treating one bright listening device as universal evidence.

5

Gentle processing trial

If your editor offers a de-esser, make a conservative processed copy and keep the original. Reject settings that make words lisp or lose detail.

6

Whole-paragraph check

Read a full original paragraph with the chosen setup. Confirm that a setting which improves one test word does not make the rest of the narration unclear.

0 of 6 drills completed. Self-review, not an audio score.

Original practice card

Use one fixed example for a fair comparison.

Original line: “Sasha sees six silver signs beside the station.” Compare untouched A with a small angle-change B. Then place it in a paragraph: “The signs point toward the east entrance. Sasha checks the map and walks on.” Listen for sharpness and for any lost consonants in both sentences.

How should the first pass work?

Read the example and limits before starting, keep a baseline, and work through the six drills above. Make two takes with a recording device you control and note the specific change privately. On playback, check that information remains intact before judging expressive choices. Stop if uncomfortable; no audio upload is needed.

Observable result

Judge the result on playback.

Check intelligibility, matched playback level, improvement on more than one device, absence of obvious processing damage, and stable results in the longer read. Reject a processed version that sounds smooth only because it erased consonants. Record the chosen position and settings so you can reproduce the comparison later.

A checked box records that you completed a step. It does not certify voice quality, skill, or professional readiness.

Limits and failure cases

Repair a failed comparison before continuing.

This page concerns recording and playback, not diagnosis or treatment of speech. Natural sibilants are part of words. Do not force tongue or jaw positions, and stop if any exercise is uncomfortable. Processing is optional and should not replace a controlled recording comparison.

Too many simultaneous changes

Changing words, level, position, and pace together makes the comparison inconclusive. Return to the example baseline and retain only the variable specified by the current drill.

Impressions instead of evidence

A more attractive sound does not establish that the task worked. Apply the page-specific playback checks, and ask a listener unfamiliar with the example to recount the information before keeping a take.

Effort used to force completion

The completion record is not an endurance contest. Stop at discomfort, shorten the session, and return to comfortable speech; pain or excessive vocal effort is never a condition for checking a box.
